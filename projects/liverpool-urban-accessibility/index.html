Liverpool workplace-flow analysis

How much recorded workplace movement stayed within Liverpool in Census 2021? An analysis of 61 statistical areas, with independent numerical checks in R.

Problem & constraints

The question is how much recorded workplace movement remained within Liverpool at Census 2021, and whether local-retention patterns varied across the city. I reduced a national origin–destination table to Liverpool’s 61 Middle layer Super Output Areas (MSOAs): Census statistical areas used to group and compare the flows. This is an analysis of recorded workplace locations, not a complete measure of transport accessibility.

My contribution

Building on my earlier R/QGIS coursework, I rebuilt the analysis using Census 2021 data. The workflow extracts the national table, checks area codes and geometry, calculates spatial measures, compares models and produces figures. I also implemented an R comparison using the same derived inputs to check selected Python calculations independently.

Approach & decisions

  1. Extract workplace flows
  2. Validate 61 areas
  3. Spatial measures & models
  4. Independent R checks
  1. Prepare the workplace-flow records with DuckDB, then join the results to geographic boundaries with GeoPandas. Check the area coverage and travel totals before interpreting the maps.
  2. Map the share of fixed-workplace flows that remain within Liverpool and use spatial statistics to investigate whether similar values cluster together.
  3. Reproduce the principal numerical results in a separate R implementation. Keep data provenance, methodology and limitations with the analysis so the path from source to finding can be inspected.

Results & interpretation

Of 123,689 included fixed-workplace flows, 84,567 had a recorded workplace within Liverpool: 68.37%. The denominator excludes the special workplace categories described in the methodology. This must not be shortened to “68% of Liverpool residents work locally”, because that changes the population being described.

The local-retention share varies across the 61 areas. A positive Moran’s I of 0.4901, with reported permutation p = 0.0001, indicates that nearby areas tended to have similar rates under the chosen spatial weights. It describes clustering in an area-level measure. It does not establish why a particular person travelled to a particular workplace.

The count-model comparison considers different assumptions about variability. On these same areas, the reported negative-binomial NB2 AIC is 711.85 versus 836.18 for Poisson. A lower Akaike information criterion favours fit relative to model complexity within this comparison. This is consistent with overdispersion in the Poisson fit; it is not a statement of causal validity.

The independent Python/R check reports a maximum coefficient difference of 1.05 × 10⁻¹² for the compared calculation. That close agreement is evidence about implementation consistency using the same inputs. Shared data limitations and modelling assumptions remain shared, even when two languages produce nearly identical numbers.

Failure cases & limits

Census 2021 was collected during pandemic disruption and does not describe present-day travel demand. Area-level associations do not establish individual behaviour or causality. ONS combines working mainly at/from home with having no fixed workplace, so that category is not a standalone home-working rate. Centroid distances simplify real routes, and area averages hide variation within each MSOA.

Engineering and verification

DuckDB queries the national table before spatial processing so the analysis carries forward the records needed for Liverpool. The workflow validates MSOA codes and geometries, then uses British National Grid for metric distances. This keeps units explicit and avoids interpreting latitude/longitude differences as distances in metres.

Queen contiguity weights describe areas as neighbours when they touch at an edge or vertex. This is a modelling choice about spatial relationships. A gravity-style workplace-accessibility measure based on a 5 km distance scale offers another view, but centroid-based distance is still a simplification rather than a measured journey route.

The Python analysis considers Poisson, negative-binomial and binomial specifications. The R script independently refits the Poisson model and reconstructs the spatial statistic. Keeping these responsibilities explicit avoids implying that every result was separately reproduced in another language.

The repository documents a verifier for saved hashes, reconstructed aggregates, model fits and fixed-seed spatial permutations. A fictional fixture allows the software path to be exercised without downloading source data.

Reading the map

Each colour shows the share of included fixed-workplace flows from an area whose recorded workplace is within Liverpool, including the origin area itself. The legend is a proportion, not a journey count or a direct measure of travel quality. A darker or lighter area should therefore be interpreted through that definition.

The map is a useful entry point because it makes the spatial pattern visible, but the overall 68.37% is calculated from the included flows. It should not be inferred by simply averaging colours or assuming that all areas contribute the same number of records. The adjacent result table gives the numerator and denominator directly.

The full-size image preserves the legend and source attribution. Census and boundary data retain their separate Open Government Licence terms. The analysis software has an evaluation-only licence, so this page describes it as public code rather than assuming that public visibility makes it open source.

Next step

Compare a newer, appropriately scoped data source and more realistic travel distances. That would require fresh provenance and careful treatment of changed definitions; the Census snapshot remains useful for its original question.
